About the Archive · 关于项目

An archive built from the ground up, in people's own words.

Most environmental history is written from policy documents and industry statistics. We start instead from the workshop floor, the village lane, the relocation site and the minefield — recording the people whose lives, bodies and landscapes are changed by decisions made far away.

  • 01
    Micro over macro

    We trace industrial change through individual work trajectories, household budgets and everyday sensory memory — not GDP tables.

  • 02
    Original voices preserved

    Quotes are kept in the narrator's own language, with translation alongside. The way a story is told is part of the evidence.

  • 03
    Public, by design

    Approved materials return to communities through exhibitions, education and this open archive — and the public is invited to collect.

  • 04
    Consent before everything

    Every narrator decides how their story is identified, used and withdrawn. Ethics is not a formality; it is the method.

Field Site 01 · 晋城阳城

When the county's heartbeat was the factory.

Yangcheng County sits in the coal-and-steel heartland of southeastern Shanxi. Since the founding of Yingchao Iron Works in 1951, its industrial park — coal, steel, building materials, chemicals and the Bagong power plant — has shaped the county's finances, its towns and, above all, the lives of two groups: industrial workers and local villagers.

Fieldwork centred on the Bagong power plant and chemical works, the old industrial district, and the factory-dependent village of Tuocun 陀村, using participant observation and one-to-one semi-structured interviews across generations.

Core industriesSteel · coal · building materials · chemicals · thermal power
Signature originYingchao Iron Works, built 1951
Two key subjectsIndustrial workers & local villagers

Three Eras · 阶段变迁

Boom, pain, renewal: a complete industrial cycle.

Boom1950s – 2000
工矿兴 · 乡土荣 — Industry rises, the countryside prospers

State-backed heavy industry expanded for decades. Villagers left farming for factory jobs — an "iron rice bowl" with monthly wages, housing, canteens, clinics and schools for workers' children.

  • Identity leap: "farmer" → "state factory worker", the most respected status in the village
  • Factory welfare system: dormitories, canteen, medical room,子弟 school, bathhouse
  • Markets and shops grew around the gates; factory and village lived in symbiosis
Pain2000s – 2018
产能收缩 · 改制波动 — Overcapacity, restructuring, decline

A single, low-end industrial structure — raw coal, rough steel, crude stone — met overcapacity, tightening environmental rules and repeated economic shocks. Old firms restructured, shrank or closed.

  • Mass layoffs, rotating shifts, wage cuts and arrears; "neither worker nor farmer" trap
  • Decades of dust, exhaust and wastewater: grey skies, heavy-metal soil, polluted wells
  • Village shops, restaurants and transport services collapsed with the factory crowds
Renewal2018 – now
生态修复 · 产业升级 — Ecological repair, industrial upgrading

Shanxi's resource-economy reform drove out backward capacity, cleaned up waste, greened old sites and brought in green smelting, new materials and circular economy industries.

  • Blue skies return; waste piles cleared; villages and plants renovated
  • New jobs demand education and digital skills — older workers are left behind
  • Hiring shifts from "employ everyone local" to "select by skill"

Subjects I · 产业工人

Workers: from pride of the village to an identity in limbo.

The factory was never just a workplace — it was a complete system of livelihood, status, time and social life. When it shrank, that system disappeared overnight.

"
Whoever in the village could get into the steel works was set for life. The state paid your wages; at festivals they gave out rice, flour and oil. Village girls all wanted to marry a factory man.
那时候村里谁能进钢厂、进矿上，就是这辈子稳了……逢年过节发米面油，村里姑娘都愿意找工厂的小伙子。
G01 · Mr. Wang, 68 — retired smelter, Yingchao Iron Works, entered 1978
"
I worked the furnace for thirty years and knew nothing but iron. When the plant closed I was nobody. I can't farm, I'm too old to migrate — high jobs won't take me, low jobs won't keep me.
干了三十年炉子，一辈子只会打铁、看火候，厂子一关，我啥也不会……高不成低不就，感觉自己废了。
G02 · Mr. Zhao, 52 — frontline smelter, laid off 2016
"
Now the factory doesn't look at seniority, only skills and certificates. Our contracts are signed year by year; when business is bad they cut staff. Sometimes I'm even sent out to pull weeds. I'm always anxious.
现在厂里不看资历，只看技术和文凭……合同一年一签，效益不好就裁员。有时候甚至只能去做除草等工作，心里一直慌。
G03 · Xiao Guo, 39 — temporary technician, new green enterprise

Three generations of workers, six conditions

What changed in working life

工人世界的四个断裂

Livelihood · 生计
Lifetime employment gave way to contract and performance work; temporary, flexible posts replaced permanent positions.

Identity · 认同
Honour and belonging became self-doubt. Older workers were brought in from hundreds of kilometres away; young workers ride motorbikes from nearby villages — status has visibly fallen.

Discipline · 生活
Fixed shifts, three rotations, canteens and courtyards collapsed into irregular days and scattered day-labour.

Generations · 代际
Old workers refuse to let their children enter the plants. The young leave through education, migration and business — the line of succession is broken.

Subjects II · 本土村民

Villagers: half-farmer, half-worker — then scattered.

At its peak, the village ran on "half farming, half industry": the old farmed, the young worked the plant, and services grew at the gates. Restructuring split the village into three very different fates.

Three fates after the boom

转型后的三类人群

① Laid-off middle-aged and elderly
Long in heavy labour with no other skills, they returned to land they had let slip. "I gave my best years to the plant; now I farm badly and no one will hire me away." — C03, Mr. Chen, 54

② Young and middle-aged migrants
They left for Taiyuan, Zhengzhou, Jiangsu and Zhejiang; hollowing and ageing set in across the village.

③ Small local business owners
Shops, food stalls and repair stores lost their customers. "Now I barely sell a few dollars a day," said the shopkeeper of 10+ years. — C04

And the cost no one sees: dense factory-village social ties — neighbours who were also workmates — have thinned into "each surviving alone, undisturbed."

Health Focus · 肺部健康

The most concrete history: what the air did to lungs.

Interviews with the Linze village clinic nurse and more than ten residents within a 1-kilometre radius trace respiratory health across the full industrial cycle. The sharpest contradiction: ordinary villagers never shared the boom, but they did share the damage.

Field Site 02 · 田山庄村

The river once fed the fields, and the fields fed the river.

Tianshanzhuang village lies in Jinze Township, Qingpu District, 60 km west of Shanghai, in the upper reaches of the Huangpu River. Its canals, rice paddies and fish ponds make it a classic Jiangnan water village — and, since the 1990s, part of the Shanghai Drinking Water Source Zone.

In spring 2011, 31 elderly farmers were interviewed in the Shanghainese dialect, for about two hours each, reconstructing a century of farming across five periods — and a lost practice of dredging river mud to feed the land.

Area · 面积4.35 km² · 1.5 km² arable, incl. 0.7 km² fish ponds
Interviews · 访谈31 elderly farmers · spring 2011
Periods · 阶段5 eras, from pre-1949 to land transfer

Five Eras · 五个阶段

A century of farming, told by the people who worked it.

B.L.Pre-1949
Before Liberation · 解放前

Yields were low, at 200–300 kg of rice per mu. Every nutrient that fed the land came from the village itself — dredged river mud, chicken and pig manure, human waste and a clover green manure.

  • About 10 boatloads of river mud per mu; labour was "free", a way of life
  • Total fertiliser input: N 7.06 kg, P 3.41 kg per mu
  • Most-honoured villagers were the farmers with the most knowledge
C.E11950 – 1958
Early Collective · 集体化早期

Land moved into mutual-aid teams and cooperatives. Labour and river mud were pooled at scale — the practice reached its peak, at about 30 boatloads per mu.

  • Total fertiliser input: N 21.10 kg, P 9.98 kg per mu
  • Periodic dredging kept canals navigable and the water clean
C.E21958 – 1983
Late Collective · 集体化晚期

People's communes and production teams organised the work. "We had to look for all kinds of fertilisers: we cut grass, got river mud, piled it to rot." The first chemical fertilisers arrived — ammonium bicarbonate and ammonia water.

  • About 15 boatloads of river mud per mu
  • Total fertiliser input: N 27.85 kg, P 12.71 kg per mu
O.P11983 – 2000
Reform · 改革开放 · 家庭联产承包

Land returned to households under the responsibility system. Chemical fertiliser became cheap and easy; river mud was "really tough", and nobody continued. Rice output peaked at 555 kg per mu.

  • River mud abandoned; total input N 18.23 kg, P 4.66 kg per mu
  • Factories and cities drew the young away; farming became part-time
O.P22000 – now
Land Transfer · 土地流转

Households rented land to grass companies and larger farms. "Almost all the young work outside, and it's impossible for them to return." The elders who hold the old knowledge are the last generation to farm.

  • Total fertiliser input: N 27.60 kg, P 4.13 kg per mu, almost all chemical
  • A health-conscious city begins to seek organic and traceable produce

Fertiliser Regimes · 肥料变迁

More nitrogen than ever — and the practice that closed the cycle.

As chemical fertilisers replaced organic ones, nutrient inputs climbed — while the one practice that took nutrients back out of the water (dredged river mud) disappeared.

What the memories tell us

The sample was deliberately weighted toward the oldest farmers, who could remember the full nutrient cycle. Women and the eldest proved the richest sources of household and sensory detail.

The loss is not simply that the knowledge is forgotten. It is that the young are unwilling to take it on — and the social status of farming has fallen.

Four Criteria · 四项条件

What it would take to bring the practice back — in their own words.

CRITERION 01 Less labour · 更省力
The new work must be no more physically demanding than other jobs — modern machinery, not strong men's shoulders.
CRITERION 02 Less dirt and smell · 更洁净
Composting and closed handling must remove the stench and the mud that repelled the young.
CRITERION 03 Better returns · 更赚钱
Combine farming with tourism, education and ecological services that the city is willing to pay for.
CRITERION 04 Honour restored · 有尊严
Make farming an honourable profession again, not a mark of low status — and pass knowledge between old and new farmers.

Field Site 03 · 武夷山国家公园

Where a mountain is governed, and its people are the keepers.

Wuyishan, in northern Fujian, is one of China's first five national parks — and the only one inscribed on the UNESCO World Heritage list as both cultural and natural. It is a layered landscape: Taoist sacred ground, the academy of Zhu Xi, tribute tea country, and a modern protected area, all in the same mountains.

Six field visits between September 2024 and May 2026 produced 35 interview participants, 23 household visits (including five oral-history households) and observations of patrols, co-management meetings, tea work and the spring mountain-calling ritual — asking how institutions, tea culture and ecology come to govern one another.

Fujian section · 福建片区1,000+ km², nearly 80% of the park
Residents · 居民28 natural villages · 3,415 registered residents
Fieldwork · 田野6 visits · 35 interviews · 23 households

A Layered Landscape · 层累的景观

Today's governance reworks many pasts, not one tradition.

SacredBefore 12th c.
Taoist & Neo-Confucian · 道教洞天 · 朱熹理学

Taoist geography numbered Wuyishan among the sixteen grotto-heavens (洞天), places where the human and immortal meet. In the Southern Song, Zhu Xi built the Wuyi Jingshe academy in 1183 and wrote and taught there for more than five decades.

TributeSong – 19th c.
Wuyi Tea · 武夷茶 · 万里茶道

Wuyi tea entered the Song tribute system. As "Bohea" it reached Europe via the Dutch East India Company and the Great Tea Road. In 1332 a mountain-calling platform was erected at the imperial tea garden, where officials and tea workers called aloud for the buds at the Jingzhe solar term.

Protected1979 – 1999
Reserve · Biosphere · World Heritage

The Wuyishan National Nature Reserve was approved in 1979. UNESCO designated it a biosphere reserve in 1987 and inscribed the mountains as a mixed cultural and natural World Heritage property in 1999.

National Park2016 – 2021
Pilot · Designation

A national-park pilot began in 2016, with formal designation as one of China's first five national parks in 2021. A multilevel community co-management committee system opened formal channels for residents' knowledge and concerns.

Recognised2022 – 2026
Tea Heritage · National Park Law

In 2022, traditional Chinese tea processing — including Wuyi rock tea — was inscribed on UNESCO's intangible heritage list. The National Park Law took effect in 2026, establishing participation, benefit-sharing and supervision for the years ahead.

Local Ecological Knowledge · 本土生态知识

Two registers of knowing: the mountain field, and the mountain's clothing.

Wuyishan's growers hold two connected vocabularies of their land — one evaluates the place that produces the tea, the other describes the vegetation that protects it.

Shanchang · 山场 (the mountain field)

A shanchang is not merely a plot but a place-specific configuration — mountain shape, rock and soil, shade, moisture, surrounding vegetation, cultivation history and sensory evaluation — that determines a tea's character.

Growers speak of the Three Pits and Two Gullies (三坑两涧), core-area rock tea (正岩), the rock rhyme (岩韵) and the rock-bone-floral-fragrance (岩骨花香). The vocabulary overlaps with the French terroir, yet it is a local Wuyi language. Lu Yu's Classic of Tea already ranked tea grown on weathered rock highest.

Cap, belt and shoes · 帽 · 腰带 · 鞋

A compact model of forest–tea relations, recurring across all five oral-history households:

  • Cap · 帽 — forest kept on hilltops and ridgelines, moderating wind, exposure and runoff
  • Belt · 腰带 — shrubs and bamboo along slopes, slowing erosion, holding moisture and habitat
  • Shoes · 鞋 — ground cover and understory protecting soil and tea roots

Tea quality and household security depend on the surrounding vegetation, not only on the planted rows.

"
This has always been our own homeland, and we have long seen ourselves as its protectors. We know this land so well that even a small change in the mountains could be noticed immediately. We had been conserving in our own way for a long time — but some customary practices were not fully recognised, and the space for us to take part became limited.
这一直是我们的家园，我们也早把自己看作守护者。对这片山熟到什么程度，山上一点小变化马上就能察觉。我们一直在以自己的方式保护，但有些老做法没被完全认可，能让我们出力的地方反而变小了。
STEWARDING · 守护 — Zhukeng Village tea farmer, March 2025
"
Wuyi tea has always been understood through shanchang. The diversity of the tea flavours comes from the environmental differences among them. The ecological environment is our rice bowl.
武夷茶一直是按山场来理解的。茶味的千差万别，来自各个山场的环境差异。生态环境，就是我们的饭碗。
KNOWING · 体认 — Tianxin Village tea farmer, October 2025
"
The co-management committee is not merely a channel for consultation. Village representatives know the terrain, the daily production and the community's needs — their feedback helps us see problems we would otherwise miss, and makes our measures more practical.
共治委员会不只是一个咨询渠道。村民代表熟悉地形、日常生产和村里的需要，他们的反馈能帮我们发现容易忽略的问题，也让管理措施更切合实际。
RECOGNISING · 接纳 — park administration official, March 2025

Three Cases · 三个案例

How the co-constitution is actually lived.

Three sites show the same relations at work — a village easement, a network of rangers, and a tea garden rebuilt on traditional principles.

Case 1 · Aotou Village bamboo easement

坳头村 · 竹林地役权 — collective ownership is retained; the park obtains fixed-period protection authority, and households accept restrictions against contract-based compensation (90,800 mu / 6,053 ha at RMB 90–149.5 per mu).
Compensation flows, 2025, Fujian section. The strongest ecological feedback was the reported return of clear downstream water.

Case 2 · Community forest rangers

社区护林员 — 80 rangers across 98 patrol grids, patrolling with drones and uploading images and reports through the park's system.
Conservation & checkpoint staff average RMB 3,130/month; rangers RMB 2,400/month.

Case 3 · Yanzike ecological tea garden

燕子窠生态茶园 — tall trees are retained for shade; soybeans and rapeseed are intercropped as green manure; local tea varieties are chosen; biological and physical pest control replaces chemicals. Temperature, humidity, PM2.5 and PM10 displays make conditions visible to managers and visitors.

"It draws precisely on traditional ecological wisdom: through human effort, a more biologically diverse environment is created — a practice where traditional culture and modern science jointly construct a modern ecological tea garden."

Jicha Hanshan · 祭茶喊山 (calling the mountain)

Around Jingzhe, the early-March "awakening of insects", tea growers offer to the tea deity Yang Taibai, beat drums and gongs, and call aloud for the buds to emerge — "茶发芽啰！" The rite accompanied Song tribute tea and was institutionalised at the Yuan imperial tea garden in 1332; today it is municipal-level intangible heritage.

Other public forms keep the knowledge visible: the seasonal Doucha tea contests (斗茶), and the outdoor production Impression Da Hong Pao. These are not unchanged survivals — the ritual is selectively translated for heritage and tourism.

"To ask the mountains for a living, one must respect them."
靠山吃山，先要敬山。 — scenic-area official, March 2025

Field Site 04 · 柬埔寨吴哥

Heritage is a complete living system — and people are part of it.

Fieldwork in Siem Reap moved between three worlds: the largest relocation community built for people moved out of the Angkor heritage zone; the restored temples, carvings and water systems of Angkor itself; and the minefields where deminers — and a Chinese drone company — confront the legacy of war.

The central question travels through all three: when conservation and conflict move people away from the places they know, who records their lives, and who bears the cost?

Relocation · 搬迁社区

AKA EK: good facilities, but the city is an hour away.

The government built a complete community for people moved out of the heritage zone — hospital, school, police station, Buddhist temple and housing blocks — with free education and healthcare. But the site sits on the outskirts: one hour to the commercial centre by car, two by motorbike, with one market and a single small factory nearby. Livelihoods fell off a cliff.

"
I trade all year and earn about 120 dollars; I can save 30. Just barely enough. The factory is an hour away by motorbike, so I gave up my better-paid job and sell by the road instead. We built our own house; when it rains badly, it leaks.
买卖一年收入约120美金，可以有30美金剩余，just barely enough……政府没有提供任何经济上的支持，严重的时候甚至会漏水。
Resident 1 · relocated 4 years ago — former factory worker, now roadside vendor
"
I used to repair roads in the city. Those contracts here all went to another construction company. The government gave only land — no housing. I sleep in my plank-board shop and hope one day they'll help build a brick house.
以前在市区做道路维修工……政府仅仅提供了土地，没有基础建筑、住房的保障，目前只能在板材搭建的商铺里睡觉。
Resident 2 · relocated 1 year ago — former road repair worker, now vendor
"
I sold fruit by motorbike in the city until my hand was badly injured in an accident. The surgery wasn't free and took most of our savings. I sell here now for very little. I hope the government can help me with a new motorbike.
手在跑摩托车途中被意外砸伤，医疗手术并不免费……花掉了阿姨家非常大一部分积蓄，希望能援助一辆新的摩托车。
Resident 3 · injured fruit seller — children's education disrupted
Residents · 三位居民

"We got land, but lost all economic security."

No financial support during relocation; homes rebuilt by hand; careers cut short; a major injury consumed the family's savings. Support, residents say, needs to be long-term and real — equal access to work, housing and a living.

Government official · 政府工作人员

"I am completely satisfied with everything."

The medical resources are excellent, the environment is beautiful, and his own child receives a good, free education. He receives a brick house, a yard and a salary supplement — he does not need to make a living. The contrast could not be sharper.

Heritage · 遗产修复

Stone remembers everything — including the restorers.

Angkor is laid out as a Hindu cosmology — moat, outer wall, gallery, inner court, central tower — later touched by Buddhism. Different countries have restored different parts, and each left a record of their philosophy on the stone itself.

And there are round holes everywhere in the walls — the unerasable memory of the Khmer Rouge. When bullet holes pass through carved myth, history shows its cruellest wound on sandstone. The Apsara became silent witnesses to human madness. When countless people fall unnamed in history, who records their lives? Who guards them?

Ecology · 生态哲学

Angkor's heritage is built from water, trees and stone together.

The temples give plants a structure to climb; trees root against the buildings and reach heights — 18–20 metres, roots 4–5 metres long — they never achieve in open forest. The jungle that grew over four centuries also shielded the site: with low bombing accuracy, almost all damage is natural collapse, not wartime destruction.

The lesson of removing trees

砍伐与保护的两次实验

Ta Prohm · 塔布隆寺
Japanese teams cut trees and even uprooted them; Indian teams later kept every tall tree, producing the famous landscape — while roots keep collapsing the stone.

Preah Khan · 圣剑寺, 2019
Crews removed a tree that was crushing a building, roots and all. Freed of support, the structure suffered far worse damage and still cannot be effectively repaired.

Open question: does taking the trees away really help the buildings? Forcing apart the human–nature harmony has produced side effects no one imagined.

Water as history

水系统承载的历史

Rivers beside the temples carried goods and people under the old dynasty; the surviving channels remain among the most complete records of daily life.

Beyond the developed temples, dense jungle and moats protect sites of living worship — at Preah Khan, believers walk barefoot over stone to the earliest carving of a ruling woman.

Heritage is not only the temple. It is a complete way of life and philosophy — and people are an indispensable part of it.

Landmines · 地雷遗产

The war did not end on the day of the ceasefire.

Cambodia is among the most mine-affected countries on earth: roughly 60,000 people have been killed or injured by leftover mines. More than a thousand deminers work today, and the 2030 mine-free goal — with huge numbers of mines still on the Thai border — is widely considered impossible.

Three demining methods, compared

人工 · 探雷鼠 · 无人机 · 1 (weak) – 5 (strong)
Rats locate mines within a 4-square-metre area, sharply cutting casualties; drones cannot work under vegetation or any cover and have unstable efficiency.

What the deminers told us

来自田野的事实

Veteran deminers — nearly 30 years on the job, some joining before the war even ended in the 1990s. The move from pure manual work to rat-assisted location was "a very big advance"; manual work in the 1990s brought constant casualties.

Scale — over 1,000 dedicated deminers. In Cambodia animals only locate; clearing still depends on human hands, unlike the fully automated systems elsewhere.

Chinese drone company — mine clearance in China is military-run with no domestic market, so they donate and train abroad, from Africa to Algeria. Field practice shows the drones' limits: no cover, no weeds, no obstacles.

Aki Ra · 排雷者

A life that is a decades-long answer to the past.

His parents died under the Khmer Rouge; as a child he was forced into the army and made to lay mines, later moving between armed groups. He speaks of those years only briefly — the silence itself shows a past that is still lived, not finished.

Today he spends his life finding and removing the very weapons he once planted — "to protect Cambodia's security and people's health." His museum turns viewing into action: tickets and coffee fund equipment, clothing and medical care for demining teams, though costs still far exceed income.

Mine victim musicians · 雷患乐队

Hundreds taking turns, playing at the temples.

At the edge of Angkor, a band of men disabled by mines — veterans of the 1990s wars — play to survive. After the government moved on the thousands of beggars, only they were permitted to perform at the site year-round.

Some cannot afford prosthetics and tap bells with simple plastic tools; hundreds rotate shifts across the temples. The question lingers: were all who were drawn into the Khmer Rouge truly beyond mercy — and what of the soldiers who had no choice?

Method · 研究方法

From memory to archive — with consent at every step.

The method is ethnographic: fieldwork, not questionnaires from a distance. Three techniques structure the work, and a five-stage process carries each story into the public archive.

Participant observation

Researchers enter workshops, old plant sites, family courtyards, village lanes and markets — observing work, routines, sociability and the texture of everyday change.

Semi-structured interviews

One-to-one interviews from tailored guides, with room to follow the narrator's own thread — recording original speech, emotion and individual stories.

Triangulation

Stories are set against photographs, maps, clinic records, gazetteers and policy timelines — so memory and document can question each other.

Step 1Story lead

Community members share memories, names or evidence — often through a volunteer collector who lives nearby.

Step 2Consent

Narrators decide how their story is identified, used and withdrawn. Anonymity is offered; sensitive questions are never forced.

Step 3Interview

Recording begins only with permission; interviews follow ethical guidelines, with simplified language for elders and low-literacy narrators.

Step 4Context

Stories are combined with photographs, maps, clinic records and historical evidence, then transcribed and checked.

Step 5Public history

Approved materials become exhibitions, education resources and this open archive — returning to the communities they came from.
